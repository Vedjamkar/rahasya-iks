People often say that ancient India had "Vedic encryption". We went to the sources to check. We found no cipher in the Vedas. We found separate practices that protected different things. Vedic recitation guards a text against changes. The Arthashastra and the Kama Sutra name secret writing but give no method. The first cipher method we found is in a commentary from the 10th to 13th century CE. Katapayadi turns numbers into words for memory, not secrecy. We sorted nine practices by the goal they serve, counted how often recitation repeats a word, and tried letter counting on a table of the same type on English. Our search was not exhaustive.

Read the full section

India had several ways of protecting knowledge. Some protected memory, some protected secrets, and some hid meaning inside symbols. Today's computing uses the same ideas under other names.

Integrity means a message has not been changed. A checksum is a modern way to check this. Confidentiality means only the right people can read a message. Encryption does this. Encoding means writing information in another form, such as numbers, so that it is easy to store or remember. ASCII is an example of encoding, and it hides nothing.

When we searched for "Vedic encryption", we found that most papers and articles put all of this under one word, "ancient Indian cryptography" [9] [10]. We did not find any cipher in the Vedas themselves. What we found is a set of different practices spread over about 2,000 years. They are Vedic recitation patterns, teaching only chosen students, the idea of parokṣa, spy messages in the Arthashastra [1], the art of secret writing in the Kama Sutra [2], the Kauṭilīya and Mūladevīya ciphers [3], Aryabhata's numerals, Katapayadi [5], and sandhyā bhāṣā.

This paper does three things. It sorts these practices by the goal each one serves (Table 1, section 5). It rebuilds some of them as working code and reports what we measured (section 7). It lists the gaps we found in the writing we read (section 8).

The figures are live. You can change the input and the result is computed again from our code, so you can check the research instead of taking our word for it.

We are computer science students, not historians. We read primary texts in translation and secondary sources, and we mark how strong each source is. We say what we could not check. The Mūladevīya table is one example. We took it from Wikipedia's version of Yashodhara's description and still need to check it against Kahn or Ganguly.

Our research statement (a hypothesis to check)Ancient Indian practices protected knowledge mainly through integrity, encoding and controlled access. The real cipher methods we found are described only from the 10th–13th c. CE, and they are weak by modern standards.
  1. Sort the main practices by the security goal they serve.
  2. Rebuild Mūladevīya and Katapayadi as working code.
  3. Test how easily a Mūladevīya-type table breaks, using frequency analysis (done on English so far; the real table on Sanskrit is still to do).
  4. Show how RSA fixes the problems the old methods had (shared table, no key).
  5. Turn all of this into an interactive research paper that students can try.

There are many claims online about ancient Indian encryption, and few of them are checked. We wanted to see which of these claims a source supports. Paper 9, for example, calls a Ramcharitmanas method "Vedic". The Ramcharitmanas is a much later text [9].

We are computer science students. That gives us something a reading list does not: we can build these systems and test them ourselves. If a cipher is a letter table, we can write the table as code and see what it does to a message.

श्रुति

७Results · Recitation

One changed word shows up 13 times in ghana.

Some recitation styles say a word more than once. In ghana, a middle word is said 13 times, so a change there shows up 13 times.

Read the full section

Vedic recitation repeats words in set patterns. Pada says each word once. Krama says words in overlapping pairs. Jaṭā and ghana go back and forth over longer runs. A recitation style "acted as a cross check on the other" [12]. We counted how many times each word is said in each style, on Ṛgveda 1.1.1, which has eight words. Choose a word in Fig. 1 to see where it falls.

Table 2. How many times each word is said, Ṛgveda 1.1.1 (8 words). Our own measurement.
StyleTotal words saidMiddle wordFirst wordLast word
Pada8111

Jaṭā42633
Ghana781353

So one changed word in the middle of the line appears wrongly up to 13 times in ghana, not once. The words at the ends get fewer checks in this count. In ghana the first word is said 5 times and the last word 3 times.

Limit: the patterns are the formulas in our research file, applied to one line, and they leave out any special end-of-line rules that reciters use. "Checksum" is our comparison to explain the idea. We do not claim that reciters were computing.

काल

७Results · The missing years

Secret writing is named early. The method we found comes centuries later.

The Arthashastra and the Kama Sutra both name it and neither says how it worked [1] [2]. How long the silence lasted depends on how you date the texts.

Read the full section

The Arthashastra says that spies used secret writing (gūḍhalekhya) and signs (saṃjñā), but it never explains how [1]. The Kama Sutra lists secret writing as art no. 44 of 64, with no method [2].

The first cipher method we found is in Yashodhara's Jayamaṅgalā commentary, from the 10th to 13th century CE. It gives the Kauṭilīya and Mūladevīya tables [3].

How long the gap is depends on the dates. Using the date ranges in Table 1, the gap after the Kama Sutra is about 300 years at the closest dates, about 800 years at the middle dates, and about 1,300 years at the furthest. So no single number is right. We do not know what the early ciphers looked like. An older one may have been lost.

७Results · Our test

Counting alone gets 36.6% right.

Our test is on English with a table of the same type. The real table on Sanskrit is still to do.

Read the full section

Every table cipher keeps the frequency of its letters. A swapped letter keeps its count wherever it goes. A table hides the letters, but it cannot change how often each one is used. So we count the letters in the swapped text, line up the commonest cipher letter with the commonest English letter, and so on down the list. Fig. 4 does this on your text.

Table 4. Letter counting alone on three English messages, each with a Mūladevīya-type table of 13 random swapped pairs. Our own measurement.

MessageLettersCorrect by counting alone
Long60136.6%
Medium24727.5%
Short9647.9%

The long message in Table 4 is the one that fills Fig. 4 when the page opens. Counting alone gets about 37% of its letters right. It does not finish the job: it gives a start, and word shapes such as "the" do the rest. Do not read much into the short message's higher percentage. Three messages are not a trend, and this is not a test of the real table on Sanskrit. If someone knows the real table, they need no attack at all, because it has no key. With 13 pairs there are 7,905,853,580,625 possible tables, and the pattern of the language still leaks through.

अंक

७Results · Katapayadi

A number is not a secret.

Katapayadi turns a word into a number so it is easy to remember. Aryabhata's numerals do the same with digits, and neither was made to hide anything.

Read the full section

Here our result is a classification, not a measurement. In Katapayadi each consonant stands for a digit, so a word or phrase stands for a number. Its earliest known use is in 683 CE (Haridatta). In Aryabhata's system a consonant is a digit and a vowel is a power of ten (c. 499 CE). Sarma's history of Katapayadi finds only two real variants [5]. Jadhav finds cryptography ideas in it but says it was not built to hide information [8]. We place both systems under "Encoding, not secrecy" in Table 1. Popular articles call Katapayadi "Vedic", but its earliest known use is 683 CE, long after the Vedic period.

कुञ्जी

७Results · The shared table

A table cipher needs a shared table. RSA does not.

Both sides of a table cipher need the same secret table. RSA (1977) lets you share a public key openly, and only the private key opens the message.

Read the full section

Every table cipher has the same weak point: both sides need the same secret table, and if the table is known, anyone can read the message. RSA (1977) removed that problem. You can share a public key openly, and only your private key can open messages locked with it. Real RSA keys are about 2048 bits long.

Fig. 6 is not secure. It uses small primes so you can follow the maths. We have not run any RSA measurement in this paper. We include it because it is the modern answer to the weak point our results point to.

Read the full section

1. The writing we found does not separate the goals. Most papers and articles we read put everything under "ancient Indian cryptography". They do not split encryption (keeping secrets) from integrity (stopping changes) and encoding (making things easy to remember). Our sorting does that. See Table 1.

2. A gap of several centuries. The Arthashastra and Kama Sutra name secret writing, but the first written method we found is in the Jayamaṅgalā commentary. Depending on the dates, the gap is about 300 years at the closest dates, about 800 at the middle dates and about 1,300 at the furthest. We do not know what the early ciphers really looked like. See Fig. 2.

3. We found no test of how strong these ciphers are. We found no paper that tries to break Mūladevīya or Kauṭilīya, for example with letter-frequency analysis. Our search was not exhaustive. Each is a fixed table with no key, so if the table is known, anyone can read the message. Our own test so far uses a table of the same type on English, not the real table on Sanskrit. See Fig. 4.

4. "Vedic encryption" claims are weakly supported. Paper 9 calls a Ramcharitmanas (16th-century) method "Vedic". Popular articles call Katapayadi "Vedic" too, but its earliest known use is 683 CE. See section 7.5.

5. Much of the writing is not peer-reviewed. It is blogs, preprints and technical reports. There are few strong academic sources. See the sources, section 2.

6. No interactive way to learn this. We found plenty of articles but no hands-on site where students can try these systems and compare them with modern ones. This paper, with its live figures, is our answer to that gap. See Fig. 1.

The same goals still exist today. Checksums protect integrity. Encryption protects confidentiality. Encoding such as ASCII only changes the form of information and hides nothing. Seeing which of these a practice actually served is the point of Table 1.

The shared-table problem is why RSA matters. A fixed table needs both sides to hold the same secret. RSA lets you share a public key openly, and it is used in HTTPS. Vedic recitation is like a checksum in the way it cross-checks itself. That is our comparison, and it does not mean the Vedas used checksums.
